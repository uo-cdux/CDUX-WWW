Particle Advection on Many-Core Clusters

Problem Overview

With this research area, we consider particle advection, i.e., displacing particles so that they are tangent to the velocity field. Particle advection is a foundational element for many visualization algorithms for flow analysis, including streamlines, pathlines, stream surfaces, and calculating Finite-Time Lyapunov Exponents (FTLE). Particle advection is a particularly difficult form of a non-embarrassingly parallel algorithm, as the work needed to complete the problem is data dependent and thus not known a priori. Further, the workload across particle advection problems can change dramatically. Streamline calculation typically involves advecting few particles for long distances, while FTLE calculation typically involves advecting many particles for short distances. Therefore, research on this problem should examine a range of scenarios, considering variation in particle count, distance traveled, and vector field. Finally, visualization and analysis is increasingly being performed in an in situ setting, where visualization and analysis is performed at the same time as the simulation, and using some of its resources. This usage modality increases the need for understanding particle advection over many architectures, and at many concurrencies.

Results

In our initial study, we compared the performance of 150 workloads on GPU clusters versus that on CPU clusters. Going into the study, we envisioned the tension to be between, on the one hand, the superior computational power of the GPU, and, on the other hand, the latencies involved with getting work from the CPU to the GPU. To our surprise, latency was a virtual non-factor. Instead, we found that the GPU often offered superior performance, but only if there was sufficient work to keep all of the GPU's processor cores busy. Alternatively, if there was relatively little work, then the CPU offered superior performance, since the processor cores on a CPU are faster than those of the GPU. (The GPU just has many more of them.)

We have followed on to this study with two additional efforts, looking at more architectures, and looking at scalability. These efforts are underway.
